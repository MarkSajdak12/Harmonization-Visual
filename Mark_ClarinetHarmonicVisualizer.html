Clarinet acoustics

A closed–open pipe

The clarinet's bore is mostly cylindrical. At the mouthpiece the reed behaves acoustically like a closed end (a pressure maximum), while the first open tone hole, or the bell, acts as an open end (a pressure minimum). A cylinder closed at one end resonates when its length is an odd number of quarter wavelengths:

fk = (2k − 1) · c / 4L  →  1×, 3×, 5×, 7× …

Only odd multiples of the lowest resonance are supported, which is why low-register clarinet tone shows strong odd and weak even harmonics. It is also why a clarinet sounds about an octave lower than a flute of similar length.

The even harmonics are never truly zero: the reed's airflow is nonlinear and generates every harmonic, the bore is not a perfect cylinder (mouthpiece, barrel, bell, tone holes), and above the tone-hole cutoff (roughly 1.5–2 kHz) open holes stop reflecting cleanly, so the odd/even pattern fades out.

Why it overblows at the twelfth

The register key opens a small vent near the top of the bore. That vent disturbs the lowest resonance much more than the next one, so the reed locks onto the next available resonance, which is the 3rd harmonic. A 3:1 frequency ratio is an octave plus a perfect fifth, a twelfth (19 semitones; 1902 cents as a pure ratio, 1900 in equal temperament).

Flutes (open at both ends) and oboes and saxophones (conical bores) support all harmonics, so they overblow at the octave instead. One register vent serves many fingerings, so its position is a compromise, which is part of why some clarion notes need adjustment.

The register break

The throat tones (written G4 to B♭4) are still lowest-resonance notes, but they use a very short sounding tube with few fingers down, and B♭4 uses the register key as a vent. They tend to be stuffier. The next note up, written B4, is the first clarion note: almost every hole closed plus the register key, sounding a twelfth above low E3.

Going from B♭4 to B4 means moving from nearly no fingers to nearly all of them at once. That transition is the break, and players speak of "crossing the break".

Clarion and altissimo spectra

In the clarion the played note sits on the bore's second resonance, so the bore's resonances no longer line up with the odd harmonics of the played note. Even harmonics become much stronger and the odd/even meter drops. Microphone recordings of clarion notes often show H3 at or above H1, helped by sound radiating more efficiently at higher frequencies; the H3 > H1 badge lights up when that happens.

How the pitch is found

This tool uses YIN: it compares the waveform with delayed copies of itself, normalizes the difference so lag zero doesn't win, and picks the first lag whose difference dips below a threshold. That lag is one period of the waveform, whatever the relative loudness of the harmonics, which is why it handles a weak clarion fundamental where "loudest FFT peak" would not.

Reading the displays

  • Noise floor hides spectrum content below it, sets the spectrogram's black level, and excludes weak harmonics from the meters.
  • Gate is a level threshold (dBFS, RMS). Below it, pitch and harmonic readouts blank out.
  • Larger FFT sizes resolve low harmonics better but smear fast changes; 16384 is a good default.
  • Demo mode plays a B♭ clarinet-style synth through all four registers and a crescendo swell.
